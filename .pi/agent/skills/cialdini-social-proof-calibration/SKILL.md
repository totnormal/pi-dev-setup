# Social Proof Calibration

## R — Reading

> “We view a behavior as more correct in a given situation to the degree that we see others performing it.”
>
> — Robert B. Cialdini, Chapter 4: Social Proof

---

## I — Methodology skeleton (Interpretation)

1. Social proof is strongest under uncertainty and when the others are similar to the audience.
2. Good proof is specific: who did what, in what context, with what result.
3. Bad proof is fake, generic, mismatched, or accidentally normalizes the unwanted behavior.
4. The agent should calibrate proof by audience similarity, evidence quality, and risk of herd effects.

---

## A1 — Application in the source

### Case 1: Laugh tracks
- **Problem**: Audiences laughed more at comedy with canned laughter.
- **Methodology use**: Even manufactured crowd response can trigger social proof.
- **Result**: Final skill must reject fake or misleading proof.

### Case 2: Bystander emergency
- **Problem**: Ambiguous emergencies can produce pluralistic ignorance.
- **Methodology use**: People look to others and may conclude inaction is correct.
- **Result**: When seeking help, make the request specific to one person.

## B — Boundary

### Do not use this skill when

- Do not use fake reviews, purchased testimonials, invented logos, or cherry-picked stats without context.
- Do not substitute social proof for product-market fit or technical proof in high-stakes decisions.
- Avoid “everyone does it wrong” messages that normalize the wrong behavior.

### Global Cialdini safety constraints

- Truthful evidence beats persuasion technique; if the evidence is weak, say so.
- Preserve meaningful choice; avoid hidden defaults, fake constraints, or social pressure that makes refusal unsafe.
- Name the influence mechanism in the answer so the user can judge it consciously.
