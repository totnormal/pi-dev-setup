# Map Attributes to Value Themes

## R — Original Passage (Reading)

> If unique attributes are your secret sauce, then value is the reason why someone might care about your secret sauce.
>
> — April Dunford, Obviously Awesome Step 6; Sales Pitch Step 5

---

## I — Methodology Skeleton (Interpretation)

Features are not value. A feature becomes useful in positioning only when it enables a benefit tied to a customer goal. Dunford’s method repeatedly asks “so what?” until the feature becomes a business outcome, then clusters related outcomes into a small number of value themes that buyers can remember.

---

## A1 — Application in the Book (Past Application)

LevelJump’s Salesforce-native architecture becomes valuable only after “so what?”: it can connect training data to sales metrics, helping sales enablement teams prove and improve revenue impact. Funnel’s data connectors become a value theme around giving marketing control over trusted data.

## E — Execution Steps

1. **Step 1**
   - List unique attributes or capabilities that alternatives lack.
   - Completion standard: this step produces a concrete, inspectable note, list, decision, or revision.

2. **Step 2**
   - For each, ask “so what?” until it becomes a customer business outcome.
   - Completion standard: this step produces a concrete, inspectable note, list, decision, or revision.

3. **Step 3**
   - Attach proof or an evidence requirement to each value point.
   - Completion standard: this step produces a concrete, inspectable note, list, decision, or revision.

4. **Step 4**
   - Cluster value points into 1–3 themes in the customer’s language.
   - Completion standard: this step produces a concrete, inspectable note, list, decision, or revision.

5. **Verification layer**
   - Verification layer: remove table-stakes capabilities and any value theme that competitors can equally claim unless your combination is uniquely defensible.
   - Completion standard: this step produces a concrete, inspectable note, list, decision, or revision.

## B — Boundary

### Do not use this skill when

- Do not confuse value with features, adjectives, or vendor opinions.
- Do not create more than three main themes for a first-call or homepage-level story.
- Do not make subjective claims without proof.

### Common failure modes from Dunford's books

- Treating positioning as copywriting rather than strategic context.
- Talking about features before clarifying alternatives and value.
- Using generic trends, generic problems, or generic proof.
- Letting the buyer infer the purchase criteria alone.
