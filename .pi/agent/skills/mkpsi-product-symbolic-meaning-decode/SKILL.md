# Decode what a product SYMBOLIZES at the unconscious level — beyond its functional attributes.

## R — Reading

> "Objects have a soul. People and products entertain a dynamic relationship of constant interaction. Individuals project themselves into products. In buying a car they actually buy an extension of their own personality."
>
> — Source: Ch. 5 "The Soul of Things"; Ch. 6 §7-10 "Things and Symbols / Connotative / Interpretive / Intentional Symbols"

**Source mechanism:** - **Recognize:** Products are not just functional objects — they carry symbolic meaning (a "soul") that drives desire at the unconscious level.
- **Interpret:** Three symbol types drive product meaning: (1) Connotative — recognizable object with manifest + latent meaning; (2) Interpretive — color, form, expression that stimulate emotional elaboration; (3) Intentional — obvious surface symbols.
- **Act:** Decode the symbolic meaning using Dichter's three principles: functional (what does it do anthropologically?), dynamic (what aspiration does it serve?), fundamental (what unconscious need does it fulfill?).

**Evidence type:** source-specific candidate extraction; evidence grade must still be checked before rollout.

## A1 — Application in the source

### Case 1: Why do men buy expensive fur coats for women? Not for warmth. Dichter traced it to the cultural-anthropological origin: the warrior bringing a skin as proof of prowess/virility.
- **Problem:** Why do men buy expensive fur coats for women? Not for warmth. Dichter traced it to the cultural-anthropological origin: the warrior bringing a skin as proof of prowess/virility.
- **Mechanism:** The source example shows the named mechanism changing perception, judgment, motivation, or behavior in a specific context.
- **Result/evidence:** Price replaces rarity as proof of the male giver's "earning virility." The fur hierarchy maps to social class: mink for society women, mouton for typists. Strategy: pull furs from the status pedestal, promote as fashion. Treat as corroborating evidence, not universal proof.
- **Transfer rule:** Apply only when the user's context matches the mechanism and can be tested with guardrails.

### Case 2: A car ad placed a mid-priced car among high-status cues (wedding, butlers, tuxedos). Respondents were hostile.
- **Problem:** A car ad placed a mid-priced car among high-status cues (wedding, butlers, tuxedos). Respondents were hostile.
- **Mechanism:** The second source example gives a cross-check against turning one anecdote into a rule.
- **Result/evidence:** The connotative symbols (luxury cues) contradicted the car's established status definition. The same car placed among "ordinary folks, vacations, driving pleasure" was accepted. Symbolic meaning must be consistent with the product's psychic content. Treat as corroborating or boundary evidence.
- **Transfer rule:** If this case conflicts with the user's context, route to `/mkpsi-orchestrator` or `/mkpsi-output-verifier`.

## B — Boundary

### Do not use this skill when

- Do not invent symbolic meanings without behavioral or interview evidence.
- Do not exploit identity, status, or inadequacy anxieties manipulatively.
- Symbolic decoding is culturally specific — meanings shift across demographics and time.
- The user only wants a book summary or author biography.
- The artifact affects high-risk contexts — minors, addiction, debt/financial hardship, health/legal/financial decisions, crisis/mental health, political persuasion, employment/housing/credit eligibility, or consent/privacy flows — unless the intervention clearly advances user welfare and preserves informed choice.

### Source-specific misuse risks

- Dichter's psychoanalytic framework (ego, id, unconscious) is historically rooted in mid-century Freudian theory; modern neuroscience offers complementary but different explanations.
- Symbolic meanings are not universal — they vary by culture, generation, and individual. Always validate with the specific audience.
