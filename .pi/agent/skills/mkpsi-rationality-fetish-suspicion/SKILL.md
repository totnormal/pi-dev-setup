# Apply systematic suspicion to "rational" explanations of behavior — they are usually rationalizations.

## R — Reading

> "Rationality is a fetish of the twentieth century; we are not allowed by our culture to admit true irrationality as an explanation of our behavior. Yet the majority of religions and political systems, as well as loyalty, love, and affection, are all irrational."
>
> — Source: Ch. 2 §3 "Fundamental Insights: the Fetish of Rationality"

**Source mechanism:** - **Recognize:** When a behavior is explained "rationally" (feature, price, convenience, habit), suspect it is a rationalization — a surface story that hides the real, often irrational, motive.
- **Interpret:** Cultural conditioning makes both researchers and consumers reject irrational explanations. This "fetish of rationality" is itself the biggest blind spot in motivation research.
- **Act:** Apply systematic suspicion: treat every "obvious" rational explanation as a hypothesis to be disproven, not a conclusion. Design experiments (behavioral, not attitudinal) to reveal the real driver.

**Evidence type:** source-specific candidate extraction; evidence grade must still be checked before rollout.

## A1 — Application in the source

### Case 1: Hundreds of people were asked what matters in coffee. They said aroma, strength, freshness, price. Nobody mentioned color.
- **Problem:** Hundreds of people were asked what matters in coffee. They said aroma, strength, freshness, price. Nobody mentioned color.
- **Mechanism:** The source example shows the named mechanism changing perception, judgment, motivation, or behavior in a specific context.
- **Result/evidence:** When served colorless coffee (purified, containing all stated qualities), most rejected it. The real driver — color — was unconscious and never stated. Treat as corroborating evidence, not universal proof.
- **Transfer rule:** Apply only when the user's context matches the mechanism and can be tested with guardrails.

### Case 2: Women were asked what they valued in soap. They said size, odor, cleaning capacity. Nobody mentioned weight.
- **Problem:** Women were asked what they valued in soap. They said size, odor, cleaning capacity. Nobody mentioned weight.
- **Mechanism:** The second source example gives a cross-check against turning one anecdote into a rule.
- **Result/evidence:** Filmed behavior showed 70% unwrapped the soap, slid fingers over it, smelled it, and weighed it in their hands. Weight was the major factor — a vestige of homemade-soap tradition — yet entirely unconscious. Treat as corroborating or boundary evidence.
- **Transfer rule:** If this case conflicts with the user's context, route to `/mkpsi-orchestrator` or `/mkpsi-output-verifier`.

## B — Boundary

### Do not use this skill when

- Do not dismiss genuinely rational analysis — some behavior IS driven by price, convenience, or feature.
- Do not replace evidence with speculation; the suspicion must lead to a test, not an assumption.
- Do not use irrationality as an excuse to manipulate; understanding the real motive carries ethical responsibility.
- The user only wants a book summary or author biography.
- The artifact affects high-risk contexts — minors, addiction, debt/financial hardship, health/legal/financial decisions, crisis/mental health, political persuasion, employment/housing/credit eligibility, or consent/privacy flows — unless the intervention clearly advances user welfare and preserves informed choice.

### Source-specific misuse risks

- Dichter sometimes overcorrected — assuming ALL rational explanations are wrong. Modern research should treat rational and irrational drivers as complementary, not exclusive.
- Behavioral experiments can be costly and ethically sensitive; design them with informed consent where possible.
